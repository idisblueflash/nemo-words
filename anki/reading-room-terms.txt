cracks	<b>say:</b> KRAKS /kɹæks/<br><b>def:</b> Cracks are thin lines where something has broken a little but hasn't split all the way apart — like on a dry sidewalk, a wall, or an old cup. As a verb, it also means a sudden sharp breaking sound or a small split appearing.<br>🔊 "cracks" rhymes exactly with "tracks"<br>📖 cracks are thin broken lines running across a surface, like tiny tracks left behind by a split<br>🎭 Old sidewalk tracks reveal a web of thin cracks.<br>Anchors: "tracks" → tracks (rhymes with cracks)<br><br><i>Nemo alternates (unreviewed):</i><br>1. Quebec	<b>say:</b> kwuh-BEK /kwəˈbɛk/<br><b>def:</b> Quebec is Canada's largest province, and the name it shares with its capital city. It's known for being mainly French-speaking — road signs, schools, and daily life run in French, unlike most of the rest of Canada.<br>🔊 Quebec ends exactly like "beck" — as in "at his beck and call"<br>📖 picture a French-speaking corner of Canada, flying its own blue fleur-de-lis flag instead of the maple leaf<br>🎭 In Quebec, one quick beck of a chef's hand orders lunch in French.<br>Anchors: "kwuh" → "quick", "bek" → "beck"<br><br><i>Nemo alternates (unreviewed):</i><br>1. platelet	<b>say:</b> PLAYT-lit /ˈpɫeɪtɫət/<br><b>def:</b> a tiny piece of blood cell that helps blood clot by sticking together to plug a cut<br>🔊 platelet = plate + the little-thing ending -let (like droplet, bracelet)<br>📖 platelets are tiny plate-shaped fragments that stack together to patch up a cut<br>🎭 Tiny plates rush to the cut, clump into a little round droplet.
tumor	<b>say:</b> TOO-mer /ˈtumɝ/<br><b>def:</b> A tumor is an abnormal lump of tissue that forms when cells grow and multiply more than they should; it can be harmless (benign) or dangerous (malignant/cancerous).<br>🔊 "tumor" rhymes exactly with "rumor" — same "-umor" ending, just swap the first letter.<br>📖 a tumor is tissue that keeps growing when it shouldn't — cells multiplying out of control, just like a rumor multiplies as it spreads.<br>🎭 Tissue spreads like a rumor.
bulge	<b>say:</b> BUHLJ /bʌldʒ/<br><b>def:</b> a part that swells or pushes outward, making a rounded bump on an otherwise smooth surface<br>🔊 "bulge" rhymes exactly with the end of indulge (in-DULGE / BULGE)<br>📖 a bulge is something pushing outward, swelling past a smooth line — like a belly straining against a shirt<br>🎭 The bulb lights my skin till the bulge bulks bigger.
apheresis	<b>say:</b> uh-FER-uh-sis /əˈfɛrəsɪs/<br><b>def:</b> A process where a machine draws your blood, separates it into parts, keeps or removes the part they want (e.g. faulty red cells), and returns the rest to you — a round trip, not just a draw. Here it's used for exchange transfusion.<br>🔊 the stressed middle sounds like "fair" — a-FAIR-esis<br>📖 a machine swaps your sickle-cell blood out and returns the rest<br>🎭 A blood fair, take off sickle cells, return the rest.
bibliography	<b>say:</b> BIB-lee-OG-ruh-fee /ˌbɪbliˈɑɡrəfi/<br><b>def:</b> A bibliography is the list of books, articles, and other sources a writer used or is recommending — usually at the end of a paper or book. If someone asks for your bibliography, they want to know where your information came from.<br>🔊 "bibliography" opens with BIB (like a baby's bib) and closes exactly like biography's "-ography"<br>📖 picture a baby's bib covered edge to edge with tiny book titles — that's the writer's own printed list of every book/source they used<br>🎭 Grandpa's bib bears tiny book titles — the sources page of his own biography.<br><br><i>Nemo alternates (unreviewed):</i><br>1. sickle	<b>say:</b> SIK-ul /ˈsɪkəl/<br><b>def:</b> An old farming tool: a short handle with a curved, C-shaped blade for cutting crops by hand. “Sickle cell” = red blood cells bent into that same crescent shape.<br>🔊 sickle rhymes with pickle<br>📖 a C-shaped curved blade<br>🎭 Cutting a C-shaped pickle with a sickle.
stiff	<b>say:</b> STIF /stɪf/<br><b>def:</b> Hard to bend or move; the opposite of soft and floppy. Can describe a thing, a body part, or a formal manner.<br>🔊 stiff rhymes with cliff<br>📖 hard, won't bend (opposite of soft)<br>🎭 Soft shoes stick well on climbing a stiff cliff.
literature	<b>say:</b> LIT-er-uh-cher /ˈlɪtərətʃər/<br><b>def:</b> Written works valued as art (novels, poems, plays); also the whole body of written material on a subject (e.g. the medical literature).<br>🔊 “little at a” (time) maps onto LIT-er-a — same rhythm and syllables; and “teacher” echoes the -ture ending /tʃər/ (litera-TURE ≈ tea-CHER).<br>📖 a body of writing you study, worked through bit by bit<br>🎭 Read a little at a time, the teacher said, on literature.
cohort	<b>say:</b> KOH-hort /ˈkoʊhɔrt/<br><b>def:</b> A defined group of people who share a trait and are treated as one set — in research, a group selected by shared characteristics and often followed over time.<br>🔊 “sort” rhymes with the “-hort” ending; “co-” like coworker/copilot<br>📖 a batch of people bundled together by a shared trait, studied as one unit<br>🎭 Co-workers in hundreds is a sort of cohort.
clog	<b>say:</b> KLOG /klɑɡ/<br><b>def:</b> To block a passage so nothing can flow through; as a noun, the blockage itself. You only clog something with flow through a narrow channel, and it usually happens by buildup.<br>🔊 rhymes with dog / log — picture a fat log in a stream<br>📖 to jam a narrow channel by buildup, so flow slows then stops<br>🎭 Chunky log clogs the narrow drain.
drain	<b>say:</b> DRAYN /dreɪn/<br><b>def:</b> (verb) To let liquid flow away and empty out; (noun) the pipe or channel it flows through. Figuratively, to use something up until it’s empty (“drain your energy,” “a drain on resources”).<br>🔊 rhymes with rain — rainwater running off down a pipe<br>📖 let liquid flow away and empty out; the channel it flows through<br>🎭 Down to the channel, rain water running off the drain.
haemoglobin	<b>say:</b> HEE-muh-gloh-bin /ˈhiməˌɡloʊbɪn/<br><b>def:</b> The substance inside red blood cells that grabs oxygen from the lungs and carries it around the body — it’s what makes blood red. Too little healthy haemoglobin means the blood can’t carry enough oxygen (anaemia).<br>🔊 “He” = HEE-, and “globe” sits in the middle (-glob-)<br>📖 moving oxygen, inside red blood cells<br>🎭 He moves an oxygen globe in the red blood cells.
lass	<b>say:</b> LASS /læs/<br><b>def:</b> A girl or young woman — a warm, informal word, heard most in Scotland, Ireland, and northern England (the word for a boy is ‘lad’).<br>🔊 rhymes with gas<br>📖 a little girl<br>🎭 A little girl ran out of gas.
thalassaemia	<b>say:</b> thal-uh-SEE-mee-uh /ˌθæləˈsimiə/<br><b>def:</b> An inherited blood disorder — you’re born with it. The body can’t make enough healthy haemoglobin, so the blood stays weak and can’t carry enough oxygen; severe cases need lifelong transfusions.<br>🔊 tha-LASS-SEE-mia — hides lass, the stressed SEE is a perfect /si/ match, and it ends in -aemia (anaemia, which is what it causes)<br>📖 inherited weak blood from birth<br>🎭 The lass sees anaemia sailor.
anaemia	<b>say:</b> uh-NEE-mee-uh /əˈnimiə/<br><b>def:</b> When you don’t have enough healthy red blood cells, so the blood can’t carry enough oxygen — you feel tired, weak, and pale. It’s the ongoing state that thalassaemia and sickle cell cause.<br>🔊 “knee” (a-NEE-mia); “need” echoes it too<br>📖 blood missing the iron it needs<br>🎭 A girl’s knee meets iron, but her blood needs it.
severe	<b>say:</b> suh-VEER /səˈvɪr/<br><b>def:</b> Very serious, intense, or extreme — the strong, dangerous end of a scale, the opposite of mild (severe illness, severe storm, severe pain).<br>🔊 -vere is /vɪr/ (VEER) → rhymes with FEAR and GEAR (fear carries the danger too). “suggest” just echoes the unstressed /sə/ start; “very” is a meaning-echo (severe = very serious), not a sound match.<br>📖 serious and dangerous — the extreme end<br>🎭 I suggest FEAR for a very dangerous GEAR.
transfusion	<b>say:</b> tranz-FYOO-zhun /trænsˈfjuʒən/<br><b>def:</b> Giving a patient donated blood (or blood parts) through a drip into a vein — topping up what their body is missing. A lifeline for thalassaemia and sickle cell patients.<br>🔊 “trans” + “fusion” (rhymes with confusion)<br>📖 donated blood transferred into the patient<br>🎭 A nurse transfers donor blood through a bedside infusion pump.
phlebotomy	<b>say:</b> fluh-BOT-uh-mee /fləˈbɑtəmi/<br><b>def:</b> Drawing blood from a vein — usually with a needle, so it can be tested in a lab or given as treatment. It’s the blood-draw step at the clinic (“getting your bloods done”).<br>🔊 phlebotomy = “f” + LOBOTOMY: /fləˈbɑtəmi/ is exactly an f in front of lobotomy /ləˈbɑtəmi/ — a perfect match across the whole back of the word. “Blood flows” supplies the leading fl- (and the blood).<br>📖 a nurse draws your blood; you watch it flow out of your vein<br>🎭 Blood flows during a lobotomy, thus a nurse draws it out.
lobe	<b>say:</b> LOHB /loʊb/<br><b>def:</b> A soft, rounded part that sticks out or forms one section of something — especially a body part. The earlobe is the soft fleshy bit hanging at the bottom of your ear; the brain, lungs, and liver are each divided into rounded lobes.<br>🔊 lobe rhymes with globe — just globe without the g<br>📖 a soft, rounded bulge — your earlobe is a tiny globe of skin<br>🎭 A soft globe under your ear.
lobotomy	<b>say:</b> luh-BOT-uh-mee /ləˈbɑtəmi/<br><b>def:</b> An old brain operation that cut the connections in the front of the brain (the frontal lobe). Once used to try to calm severely disturbed patients, it often left them dull and emotionless — now seen as a terrible mistake. Also used loosely: “like a lobotomy” = left blank and switched-off.<br>🔊 hides BOTTOM — lo-BOT-omy — a perfect /bɑtəm/ match (same trick as phle-BOT-omy); and it rhymes with phlebotomy — just drop the f.<br>📖 cutting the brain’s LOBE (lobo-) until the mind is scraped down to the bottom, blank and empty<br>🎭 Cutting the brain’s LOBe down to BOTtom meat.
congenital	<b>say:</b> kun-JEN-ih-tul /kənˈdʒɛnətəl/<br><b>def:</b> Present from birth — a condition you’re born with, not something you catch or develop later. Usually written in your genes and there from day one.<br>🔊 con-GEN-ital carries “gen”, like genes<br>📖 it comes from your genes — present from birth, not caught later<br>🎭 Comes from genes, told at birth.
polymerize	<b>say:</b> puh-LIM-uh-rize /ˈpɑlɪmərˌaɪz/<br><b>def:</b> To join many small identical units into one long chain. In sickle cell, deoxygenated haemoglobin (HbS) molecules polymerize — they stack into long stiff rods that bend the red cell into a sickle.<br>🔊 “polymerize” = polish + more + eyes<br>📖 many small units link up into one long chain<br>🎭 Polish more drops, chain together from your eyes.
hydrophobic	<b>say:</b> hy-druh-FOH-bik /ˌhaɪdrəˈfoʊbɪk/<br><b>def:</b> Water-repelling — a surface or patch that avoids and won’t mix with water, so it pushes water away and seeks other oily/hydrophobic patches instead. That’s why the sticky valine patch on deoxygenated HbS latches onto its neighbour.<br>🔊 “hydro” = water (like hydrant); “-phobic” = fearing (like phobia)<br>📖 water-fearing → repels water, sticks to other oily patches<br>🎭 A hydrant's water-phobic — that's me!<br>🎭 pertaining to fear of water
paucity	<b>say:</b> PAW-sih-tee /ˈpɔsəˌti/<br><b>def:</b> A very small amount — a shortage or lack of something. A slightly formal word for "not nearly enough" (a paucity of evidence, a paucity of jobs).<br>🔊 "Pause" + "city" → PAW-city = paucity<br>📖 the water is paused — only a trickle escapes, never enough<br>🎭 The dog's paw scrapes the city's dry well, water lacking.
tacrolimus	<b>say:</b> tak-roh-LY-mus /ˌtækroʊˈlaɪməs/<br><b>def:</b> A medicine that calms down the immune system on purpose, so the body won't attack (reject) a transplant. Given as a drip (IV) at first, then usually switched to a pill (oral).<br>🔊 four beats rebuild the whole word: tackle · row · lime · us (tac-ro-lime-us) → /ˌtækroʊˈlaɪməs/<br>📖 the drug tackles a row of immune guards, then "limes" the new organ green (camouflage) so it slips past unseen<br>🎭 The drug tackles a row of immune guards, then limes us a green light.
tackle	<b>say:</b> TACK-ul /ˈtækəl/<br><b>def:</b> To grab something head-on and bring it down; or to take on a hard job with full effort (tackle a runner; tackle a problem).<br>🔊 "tackle" starts like tax (TAX ≈ TACK-)<br>📖 grab the big one and bring him down — that's a tackle<br>🎭 Tax officer grabs the rich guy and brings him down.
allogeneic	<b>say:</b> al-oh-je-NAY-ik /ˌæloʊdʒəˈneɪɪk/<br><b>def:</b> Coming from a different person — a donor, not your own body. In an allogeneic transplant the cells or organ come from someone else. (Opposite: autologous — from your own body.)<br>🔊 Aloe · gel · nay · nick rebuilds a-lo-ge-NAY-ic (allo- is exactly aloe /æloʊ/)<br>📖 a donor's nick — a small graft taken from another person, not your own body<br>🎭 Aloe gel? Nay, a donor's nick.
donor	<b>say:</b> DOH-ner /ˈdoʊnər/<br><b>def:</b> Someone who gives something away to help another — blood, an organ, money, time. In a transplant, the person the new organ or cells come from.<br>🔊 donor = "d" + owner (owner rhymes exactly and hides inside it)<br>📖 an owner who has died signs his kidney over to someone else — that's a donor<br>🎭 The dead owner donated his kidney.
aloe	<b>say:</b> AL-oh /ˈæloʊ/<br><b>def:</b> A plant with thick, spiky green leaves full of clear gel. The gel is rubbed on skin to cool and soothe burns and sunburn (aloe vera).<br>🔊 "shall" /ʃæl/ echoes the AL- front of aloe (rhymes with pal, gal, shallow)<br>📖 a spiky green plant whose cool gel soothes a burn<br>🎭 Shall I cut the low plant for gel?
nay	<b>say:</b> NAY /ˈneɪ/<br><b>def:</b> An old-fashioned or formal word for "no" — heard in votes ("the nays have it") and older English. It sounds exactly like "neigh," the noise a horse makes.<br>🔊 homophone of "neigh" (a horse's sound); rhymes with day<br>📖 an old word for "no" — a "nay" vote is a vote against<br>🎭 A native says "nay" for no.
transplants	<b>say:</b> trans-PLANTS /trænsˈplænts/<br><b>def:</b> A transplant moves something living from one place into another — most often an organ (heart, kidney, marrow) taken from a donor and placed into a patient who needs it. Can also mean moving a plant to new soil. Either way: lift it from here, settle it there.<br>🔊 "transplants" literally ends in plants — trans + plants<br>📖 trans- = across; you move a living plant (or organ) across, into a new home<br>🎭 Transfer the plant alive!
phobic	<b>say:</b> FOH-bik /ˈfoʊbɪk/<br><b>def:</b> Intensely afraid of or unable to stand something, enough to actively avoid it; also a suffix meaning "fearing ___" (hydrophobic, claustrophobic).<br>🔊 "phobic" starts exactly like foe (/foʊ/) + "bic"<br>📖 if you're phobic about something, you treat it like a foe — an enemy to avoid<br>🎭 A terrible photo gets big and thick in your brain.
hydrant	<b>say:</b> HY-drunt /ˈhaɪdɹənt/<br><b>def:</b> the red pipe on a street corner that firefighters connect a hose to for water during a fire<br>🔊 "hydrant" rhymes with "front" (-drant / -front)<br>📖 a hydrant is the red pipe on the corner that gives firefighters water<br>🎭 A high-speed car drives front into a hydrant.
optimal	<b>say:</b> OP-tih-mul /ˈɑptəməɫ/<br><b>def:</b> the best possible choice given the situation — not just good, but the single best option once every limit and trade-off is factored in.<br>🔊 optimal starts with opt — same sound, dead-on, /ɑpt/.<br>📖 you opt (choose) the optimal thing when it beats every other option on the table.<br>🎭 Select the Option — not the minimal, not the maximal, but the optimal balance — of all.
regime	<b>say:</b> ray-ZHEEM /ɹeɪˈʒim/<br><b>def:</b> The system or plan in charge of running something — most often a government in power, but also used for a structured treatment plan (e.g. "immunosuppression regime" = the set of drugs/schedule used to suppress the immune system).<br>🔊 "regime" starts with ray and ends rhyming with team<br>📖 picture a team that seized control and now calls all the shots — that's the ruling regime (or, medically, the plan a treatment team follows)<br>🎭 Take an X-ray before any plan of the treatment team.
undergone	<b>say:</b> un-der-GONE /ˌəndɝˈɡɔn/<br><b>def:</b> To go through something, usually a difficult, long, or big experience, from start to finish.<br>🔊 "undergone" splits exactly into two words you already know — "under" + "gone"<br>📖 to undergo something is to go under it and come out the other side — it's gone through, finished<br>🎭 Under plastic surgery, her small eyes were gone.
inpatient	<b>say:</b> IN-pay-shunt /ˈɪnˌpeɪʃənt/<br><b>def:</b> someone who stays overnight or longer in a hospital to be treated (opposite of outpatient, who visits and goes home same day)<br>🔊 "inpatient" is just "in" + "patient" — no trick needed, it's built from words you already know<br>📖 an inpatient is a patient who stays in the hospital, not just visiting<br>🎭 In hospital, checked in and stayed in — you won't be just a patient.
malignancy	<b>say:</b> muh-LIG-nun-see /məˈlɪɡnənsi/<br><b>def:</b> when something growing in the body — usually a tumor — is dangerous, out of control, and can spread to other parts of the body<br>🔊 "malignancy" is just "malignant" with "-ancy" tacked on the end<br>📖 the "mal-" at the front means bad (like malicious, malfunction) — a malignancy is a bad growth<br>🎭 a machine rigs big ants across the body.
malignant	<b>say:</b> muh-LIG-nuhnt /məˈlɪɡnənt/<br><b>def:</b> cancerous — growing and spreading through the body in an uncontrolled, harmful way<br>🔊 machine (mə) + rig (rhymes with lɪɡ) + ants (~nənt) → machine-RIG-ants<br>📖 malignant cells spread uncontrollably through the body, like ants crawling everywhere<br>🎭 A machine rigs big ants that spread across the body.
limb	<b>say:</b> LIM /lɪm/<br><b>def:</b> an arm or a leg (or a tree branch) — a part of the body that sticks out and lets you move<br>🔊 "limb" opens with the same /lɪm/ sound as "limit"<br>📖 a limb is an arm or a leg — the body parts you stretch at the gym<br>🎭 Limit your arm and leg stretching in the gym.
autologous	<b>say:</b> aw-TAH-luh-gus /ɔːˈtɑːləɡəs/<br><b>def:</b> from your own body, given back to yourself — not from or to someone else<br>🔊 "autologous" breaks into AUTO + LOG + US<br>📖 from yourself, for yourself — a private loop, self to self<br>🎭 Otto logs his own bone marrow, returned only to himself, never to us.
steelman	<b>say:</b> STEEL-mun /ˈstiɫmən/<br><b>def:</b> to state the opposing argument in its strongest, most convincing form before you respond to it — the opposite of a strawman<br>🔊 "steelman" is just "steel" + "man" run together<br>📖 you build the opposing argument out of steel — the toughest, strongest version — instead of flimsy straw<br>🎭 Build a steelman for your opponent, then fight it fair.
dismissal	<b>say:</b> dis-MISS-ul /dɪsˈmɪsəɫ/<br><b>def:</b> waving something off as not worth taking seriously, without really engaging with it<br>🔊 "dismissal" is just "dismiss" with "-al" tacked on the end<br>📖 to dismiss is to wave something off — "not worth my time"<br>🎭 Dismiss silly questions along the way home.
dismiss	<b>say:</b> dis-MISS /dɪsˈmɪs/<br><b>def:</b> to wave something away as not worth your attention, or to send someone away<br>🔊 "dismiss" splits into two familiar words: "dis" + "miss"<br>📖 to dismiss is to wave something off — "not worth my time" — or send someone away<br>🎭 In Disco, the teacher sent Miss home.
marrow	<b>say:</b> MEHR-oh /ˈmɛɹoʊ/<br><b>def:</b> the soft substance packed inside bones, known for making blood cells<br>🔊 marrow rhymes with sparrow (just drop the initial "s")<br>📖 marrow is the soft stuff packed deep inside a bone<br>🎭 More soft sparrows fly through the bone's hole.
myeloma	<b>say:</b> my-uh-LOH-muh /maɪəˈloʊmə/<br><b>def:</b> A cancer of plasma cells that grow out of control in the bone marrow, crowding out healthy blood cells and weakening bone.<br>🔊 myeloma hides "loam" in its middle — my-LOAM-uh<br>📖 bone marrow is like rich loam where blood cells grow; in myeloma, cancer cells take root there and crowd everything else out<br>🎭 My rich loam boosts the weed cells and brings more sparrows (marrow).
plasma	<b>say:</b> PLAZ-muh /ˈplæzmə/<br><b>def:</b> the pale yellow liquid part of blood that carries blood cells, nutrients, and proteins around the body<br>🔊 "plasma" is just "plaza" with an extra "m" tucked on the end<br>📖 picture a town plaza flooded with pale yellow liquid — that flood is what carries the blood cells across<br>🎭 Pale yellow water floods the plaza with blood cells.
loam	<b>say:</b> LOHM /ˈɫoʊm/<br><b>def:</b> soft, dark, rich soil that's great for growing plants<br>🔊 "loam" rhymes with "home"<br>📖 loam is the soil where roots feel most comfortable — rich, soft, welcoming<br>🎭 Lots of soft, dark soil in my homeland.
sparrow	<b>say:</b> SPEH-roh /ˈspɛɹoʊ/<br><b>def:</b> a small, plain, common brown bird<br>🔊 sparrow splits into spare + row<br>📖 a small, plain, everywhere-bird — nothing showy about it<br>🎭 On a spare row of chairs, a small brown bird lands.
spare	<b>say:</b> SPAIR /spɛɹ/<br><b>def:</b> extra, kept unused just in case<br>🔊 spare rhymes with chair<br>📖 extra, sitting there unused, just in case<br>🎭 I set up another chair aside, just in case.
corpus	<b>say:</b> KOR-pəs /ˈkɔɹpəs/<br><b>def:</b> a large body/collection of text or writing gathered together for study or search<br>🔊 "corpus" splits into "cor-" (echoed by "Four" — same -or sound) and "-pus" (echoed by "purple bus" — near-rhyme like "campus")<br>📖 a corpus is a huge body of text you can search — like a king ruling over mountains of text<br>🎭 The King of Four, handling mountains of text in a purple bus.
pate	<b>say:</b> PAYT /peɪt/<br><b>def:</b> the top of someone's head, especially when bald — often used humorously<br>🔊 pate rhymes exactly with plate<br>📖 your pate is the top of your head — the "plate" up top<br>🎭 He balanced a plate on his bald pate.
